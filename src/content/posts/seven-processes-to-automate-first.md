Automation is most useful when it removes friction from a process that already has a clear purpose. It is less useful when it simply makes a confusing process run faster.

A good first automation normally has four characteristics: the work happens often, the inputs are reasonably consistent, the rules can be explained, and a person can step in when something unusual occurs.

## 1. New enquiry capture and routing

A customer enquiry may arrive through a website form, shared inbox, social message or booking tool. Someone then has to read it, identify what it is about, copy the details into another system and decide who should respond.

A controlled workflow can:

- capture the enquiry in one place;
- extract the contact, company, service and deadline;
- assign a category or priority;
- create a record in the customer relationship system;
- alert the right team member; and
- send an approved acknowledgement.

Keep a person involved when the request is sensitive, unclear or high value. The objective is faster organisation, not an unreviewed promise to the customer.

## 2. Appointment reminders and follow-up

Missed appointments and inconsistent follow-up cost time. A simple workflow can send reminders before a meeting, provide preparation instructions and create a follow-up task afterwards.

The workflow should respect communication preferences and avoid repeatedly contacting someone who has cancelled or opted out.

## 3. Recurring management reports

Many teams spend part of every week copying numbers from several tools into a spreadsheet or presentation. Automation can gather approved data, calculate the same measures and create a draft summary.

A person should still investigate unexpected results and explain the business context. Automating collection gives them more time to interpret rather than assemble.

## 4. Document intake and data entry

Standard documents such as enquiry forms, supplier invoices or application forms often contain information that needs to be entered into another system.

An automation can read the document, extract defined fields and flag missing or uncertain values for review. It should never silently guess when the data is ambiguous. Confidence checks and an exception queue make the workflow safer.

## 5. Customer support triage

A shared support inbox can be sorted by topic, urgency, customer or required team. For common questions, the system can retrieve an approved knowledge article or prepare a draft response.

Avoid allowing a model to invent policy, pricing or commitments. Use a controlled information source and route complaints, payment issues, vulnerable customers and unusual requests to a person.

## 6. Content preparation and approval

AI can help turn an approved brief into a first draft, suggest variations or reformat one idea for several channels. That does not mean it should publish by itself.

A reliable content workflow separates these stages:

1. brief and source material;
2. first draft;
3. factual and brand review;
4. stakeholder approval;
5. scheduling; and
6. publication record.

This keeps the speed advantage of drafting while preserving accountability. The Inovantage website includes the same review-first principle for insight posts: content remains unpublished until an authorised editor approves it.

## 7. Internal task handoffs

Work often slows down between departments rather than within them. An automation can create the next task, attach the relevant information, set an owner and send an alert when a stage is complete.

Examples include a signed proposal moving into onboarding, an approved design moving into development, or a completed project triggering an invoice request and feedback email.

## A simple scoring method

List ten repetitive processes and score each from one to five for:

- frequency;
- time spent;
- delay caused;
- consistency of inputs;
- clarity of rules; and
- business value of improvement.

Then subtract points for high data sensitivity, poor source data, unclear ownership and severe consequences if the workflow fails. The best first project is not always the biggest. It is usually the one that produces visible value with manageable risk.

## Define the human checkpoints

Before building, write down:

- what starts the workflow;
- which data it may use;
- which decisions are rule-based;
- which decisions require approval;
- what happens when information is missing;
- who receives an alert when something fails; and
- how outcomes will be measured.

A well-designed automation does not hide responsibility. It makes the routine path faster and the exception path clearer.

## Start with one measurable result

Choose an outcome such as reducing enquiry response time, removing two hours of weekly reporting, or cutting repeated data entry. Run the workflow on a limited set of cases, review the logs and improve it before expanding.

That approach produces a system the team understands and trusts, rather than an impressive demonstration that becomes difficult to operate.
