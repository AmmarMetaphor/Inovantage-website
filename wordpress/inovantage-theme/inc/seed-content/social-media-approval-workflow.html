Social media becomes stressful when ideas, copy, images and approvals are scattered across messages and spreadsheets. A simple workflow gives every post one owner, one status and one approved version.

The aim is not to create bureaucracy. It is to remove last-minute uncertainty and prevent content from being published before the right person has reviewed it.

Stage 1: Agree the content plan

Start with a monthly or fortnightly plan that identifies:

  • the business objective;
  • priority audiences;
  • content themes;
  • important dates;
  • offers or campaigns;
  • channels; and
  • the person responsible for final approval.

A content calendar should contain enough information to explain why each post exists. A title such as “Tuesday post” is not a useful brief.

Stage 2: Write a complete brief

For every planned post, record:

  • the message or question;
  • source information;
  • desired action;
  • channel and format;
  • required visual assets;
  • claims that need verification;
  • links and tracking information; and
  • deadline.

When AI is used to create a draft, the approved brief and source material should guide it. Do not ask a model to invent customer facts, results or policies.

Stage 3: Create the draft

Prepare the caption and creative together so the reviewer sees the full post rather than disconnected parts. Include alternative text when an image communicates information.

Use a clear status such as Draft. At this stage, the creator may still be checking facts, links, spelling, dimensions and brand presentation.

Stage 4: Run an internal quality check

Before requesting approval, check:

  • Is the main point clear in the first lines?
  • Is the claim supported by an approved source?
  • Does the tone sound like the brand?
  • Is the call to action accurate?
  • Are names, dates, prices and links correct?
  • Does the visual match the final caption?
  • Is the content accessible?
  • Are there privacy or confidentiality concerns?
  • Could the wording be misunderstood without additional context?

The creator should resolve routine issues before asking a senior stakeholder to review.

Stage 5: Request review in one place

Move the post to In review and notify the named approver. Ask reviewers to add comments to the content record rather than sending competing versions through email and chat.

Set a reasonable review deadline. When feedback conflicts, one person needs authority to decide which direction to follow.

Stage 6: Record approval

After changes are complete, the approver should mark the exact version as Ready or Approved. The record should show who approved it and when.

Approval should cover both copy and creative. Changing the message after approval requires another review when the change is material.

Stage 7: Schedule and verify

Schedule the approved version using the selected publishing tool. Check the final preview for line breaks, image crop, tags, link destination and publication time.

The scheduler should not have to interpret which file or caption is current. There should be one approved source.

Stage 8: Monitor and learn

After publication, check for customer questions, errors or sensitive responses. Record useful measures in a simple report and note what should change in the next cycle.

Do not judge every post on reach alone. The right measure depends on the objective: awareness, website visits, enquiries, event registrations, recruitment or customer support.

A practical status board

A small team can use five statuses:

  1. Idea — approved for development but not written.
  2. Draft — copy or creative is being prepared.
  3. In review — complete version is awaiting comments or approval.
  4. Ready — final version is approved for scheduling.
  5. Published — live link and date are recorded.

Limit who can move content from Ready to Published. This creates a deliberate final control without slowing the rest of the process.

Define urgent-content rules

Occasionally, a post needs to be created quickly. Agree the exception process in advance:

  • who may request urgent content;
  • who must approve it;
  • which checks can never be skipped;
  • how the decision is recorded; and
  • who monitors replies after publication.

Urgency should shorten the route, not remove accountability.

Use AI as an assistant, not the approver

AI can help generate alternatives, summarise source material, adapt a post for another channel or identify possible ambiguities. It cannot accept responsibility for accuracy, confidentiality or brand risk.

The strongest workflow combines fast drafting with named human review. That is how a team can publish more consistently without giving up control.
